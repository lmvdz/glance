# Brief for a graphic artist

You're being asked to make a piece about an idea. You don't need any technical background. Read
this, understand what the idea feels like, and then make whatever you believe brings it into the
world. Nothing below tells you how it should look. That part is yours.

## The situation

There are now software workers, AI agents, that work on long projects: days, weeks. They work in
shifts, many of them in parallel, and each one has a strange affliction: at the end of its shift,
its mind is destroyed. Completely. Whatever it was holding in its head, whatever it learned,
whatever it decided, is gone unless it was written down somewhere outside itself.

So the entire question of whether these workers can do long, serious work comes down to one thing:
what gets written down, how, and whether the next worker can trust it.

## The idea

The people building these systems mostly got this wrong at first. They built the equivalent of a
giant pile of loose notes and a very good search engine over the pile. Ask the pile a question and
it hands you the five notes that sound most similar to your question. The problem: the pile
contains both the old plan and the new plan that replaced it, and they sound almost identical. The
search engine hands you both with equal confidence. The worker picks one at random. Sometimes it
acts on a decision that was reversed a week ago, confidently.

The idea we believe in is much older than computers. It is the ledger.

An accountant never erases. A mistake gets a single line drawn through it, so it stays readable
forever, and a correction is written beneath it, dated and signed. Nurses' charts follow the same
law: never erase, strike through, initial it. The record of being wrong is preserved alongside the
correction. Because of that, you can open the book to any past day and know exactly what was
believed on that day, and you can always tell what is current from what is history.

That's the whole idea, applied to machine memory:

- Everything that happens is written down once, permanently, in order. Nothing is ever deleted.
- When a fact is replaced, the old one is struck through, not removed. Still legible. Still there.
  But no longer the truth.
- What a new worker gets at the start of its shift is not the pile and not the whole book. It's a
  short, current briefing, rebuilt fresh from the record, the way a night nurse hands off to the
  morning nurse: here's the state of things, here's what's pending, here's what needs you. Not
  eight hours of narration. And every line of that briefing points back to the page in the book
  that proves it.
- If a worker dies mid-shift (they do, constantly), nothing it knew is truly lost, because the
  record was being kept outside its head all along. Someone reconstructs its handoff from the
  book, and the work continues.

## Why it matters, emotionally

This is really a piece about trust and continuity. About how anything, a hospital, a ship's crew,
a monastery, a relay of short-lived workers, can hold knowledge for longer than any individual
mind lasts. The individual forgets everything; the institution remembers, because of a discipline
about writing things down. Memory here isn't a warehouse to search. It's a practice: a book that
is always current, always honest about what used to be true, and always able to prove itself.

And there's something quietly beautiful in the central rule: **nothing is ever erased, and yet
only one thing is ever true.** The struck-through line is not garbage. It's the visible history of
changing your mind, kept forever, right next to what replaced it.

## What the piece is for

It will accompany a written position paper arguing for this approach, shared with engineers and
researchers. It can be a hero image, a poster, a standalone illustration. One piece or a small
series, your call.

## What we are not telling you

Style, medium, palette, mood, whether it's literal or abstract, whether machines appear in it at
all. We have our own diagrams already; we don't need another diagram. We need what you see when
you understand the idea.
